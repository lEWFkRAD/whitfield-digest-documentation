Institutional map

No single official controlled the whole system

Valuation

Board of Tax Assessors

Independent local valuation body; appraisal staff prepare parcel values and the digest.
Billing

Tax Commissioner

Signs the digest, bills taxpayers, collects, refunds, and distributes taxes. Does not set parcel values.
Review

Georgia DOR

Reviews digest compliance, millage submissions, ratios, and approval status.
Continuity

Superior Court

Resolved the signature dispute and authorized temporary collection during the impasse.
Keep the lanes straight: valuation, billing, state review, and judicial continuity are different functions. A breakdown between them is not proof that one actor caused all resulting costs.

March–August 2024

The disputed intervention—and the refusal

March 2024 • a percentage limit was requested

Gail Noles later said unnamed elected officials asked the former assessor-board chairman for a set percentage increase. She said the board accepted it and compressing completed values created “unexpected consequences.”

Later meeting • Jensen suggested “a small increase”

Jevin Jensen said he and Dalton Mayor Annalee Harlan Sams met with assessors. The reporting does not prove they were the unnamed March participants.

June 21–24 • Sane refused to sign

Reporting quotes Sane saying 800+ commercial/industrial accounts fell by more than $50,000 while 7,000+ homes rose by more than 50%.

August 21–29 • mandamus

The assessor board sought a court order. A visiting judge reportedly compelled Sane to perform the ministerial act of signing.

Year-end close material weakness

Material adjustments and prior-period restatements were required. Separate findings covered segregation of duties, void approvals, and deposit delays.

This was not yet the digest-specific restatement finding.

Digest reconciliation material weakness

The audit says temporary-versus-final digest differences were not identified and corrected timely. Management began reconciliation after audit inquiries.

The honest conclusion

What can be said—and what cannot

Supported now

  • A genuine digest and billing breakdown occurred.
  • Political pressure over assessment levels was publicly acknowledged.
  • Temporary court-authorized billing was required.
  • Every June 2024 assessment notice was replaced.
  • Refund funding and audit corrections followed.
  • The delay created documented TAN cash-flow pressure.
  • General Fund capacity fell materially by FY2025.

Not supported now

  • The identities of the unnamed March participants.
  • Criminal conduct, fraud, or another specific illegality.
  • A recruitment-motive theory.
  • That all reserve decline was digest-caused.
  • That temporary billing itself equals overbilling.
  • A complete gross-to-net digest loss.
  • That TAN principal was a loss.
Discipline: absence of proof is not proof of absence; these are current evidence limits, not exonerations or accusations.

Method, limits, and handoff

A documented public-record review—not an audit opinion

What was done

Official records were downloaded and inspected; exact cells/pages were recorded; arithmetic was independently reproduced; reporting and allegations were kept in separate evidence classes.

What was not done

No county GL, parcel-level roll, refund register, DOR case file, March recording, or signed Sane letter was available. No interviews were treated as sworn evidence.

How to update

Add new records to the source register, update the claims matrix first, then revise the deck. Never overwrite the evidence class simply because a narrative becomes more persuasive.

Resident takeaway: Whitfield County’s 2024 digest breakdown is real, consequential, and documented. The remaining job is to quantify the digest-specific effects and identify who requested what—without turning unanswered questions into accusations.
